Process Over Product: Faculty Workbook

Work through Skill, Spot, Step, and Score on one assessment you already use. Protect what the objective names, open what it doesn't, and put the points on the reasoning.

Your work saves only in this browser. Nothing is sent anywhere. Use the summary page to print or copy your plan.

    Your assessment

    Start with one Canvas quiz, discussion, or assignment you plan to teach again. Paste its learning objective exactly as written: the verbs will matter.

    Want to see a finished example first? Load the vitamin D handout from the workshop, then clear it when you're ready to start your own.

    1. Skill: split the task

    List every operation a student performs to complete this assessment, including the ones that feel like busywork. Then ask two questions of each one.

    Question 1: Is this operation, or the reasoning behind it, named in the learning objective?
    Question 2: Would a student have to do it unaided later: on the final, in clinical or on the job, or in the next course?
    Weight is a judgment, not a formula. Peripheral work may be opened to AI; nothing has to be.

    2. Spot: place the protected work on the ladder

    For each protected operation, mark where it sits now and where one revision could take it. The ladder measures how much of what the work needs only the student has. Higher rungs make the student's thinking necessary and visible; none of them is "AI-proof."

    3. Step: choose one revision

    Pick the smallest change that makes the protected work necessary and visible. Options that fit your assessment type and target rung are shown first.

    4. Score: put the points on the reasoning

    Rubric points are an incentive. When most of the points sit on the finished product, offloading the reasoning becomes the rational choice. Assign percentages so the protected work carries the weight.

    Make generic answers lose points

    Add a criterion like this to any process piece. It is the line that makes the process required, not optional.

    Specific account of a check, rejection, doubt, or revision that points to something in the student's own work: a quoted sentence, a named claim, a named source. A response that could be pasted unchanged into another student's paper scores 0.
